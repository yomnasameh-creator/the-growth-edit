SEO for Beginners: How Search Engines Work

Learn how search engines find, store and rank pages, so you know what you can influence and what you cannot.

By THE GROWTH EDIT Published November 26, 2026

When you type a question into a search engine, an organized list of pages appears in less than a second. It can feel like magic, but it is the result of a process that follows the same basic steps every time.

SEO, short for search engine optimization, is the practice of making your website easier for that process to understand and more useful to the people it serves. This guide explains how search engines work in plain English, so every SEO tip you read later will make more sense.

What a search engine is trying to do

A search engine has one main job: to give each person the most helpful results for what they are looking for. To do this, it has to do three things well. It must find pages, understand them, and choose which ones to show first. These three jobs are usually called crawling, indexing and ranking.

Step 1: Crawling

Search engines use automated programs, often called crawlers or bots, to discover pages. A crawler visits a page, reads it, and follows the links on it to find more pages. Over time it moves across the web from link to link.

This has some practical lessons for you:

  • Links help pages get found. A page that no other page links to is harder for a crawler to discover. Linking your pages to each other, called internal linking, helps.
  • A sitemap can help. An XML sitemap is a file that lists the pages you want search engines to know about.
  • You can give instructions. A file called robots.txt tells crawlers which areas of your site they should not visit. Use it carefully, because blocking the wrong area can hide pages you want people to find.

Step 2: Indexing

After a page is crawled, the search engine tries to understand it. It looks at the text, headings, images, links and the page title, and decides what the page is about. It may then store the page in a huge database called the index.

Not every crawled page is indexed. A page might be left out if it has very little useful content, if it is a near-copy of another page, if it is marked with a "noindex" instruction, or if there are technical problems. Being in the index is a requirement for appearing in results, but it is not a guarantee of being shown.

Step 3: Ranking

When someone searches, the search engine looks through its index and decides which pages to show and in what order. This is ranking. Broadly, search engines try to judge:

  • Relevance: does the page match what the person is looking for?
  • Quality: is the content helpful, clear and trustworthy?
  • Usability: does the page work well, including on a phone?

Search engines do not publish every detail of how they rank pages, and their systems change over time. That is why nobody can honestly promise you a top position. Be careful with anyone who does.

Understanding search intent

Search intent is the reason behind a search. The same topic can lead to very different needs. Four common types are:

  • Learning: "how do search engines work"
  • Finding a specific site or place: a brand name or a local business name
  • Comparing options: "best ways to promote a small business"
  • Taking action: searching with the aim of buying or booking something

Good SEO content matches the intent. If people searching a phrase want a simple explanation, a long sales page will not satisfy them. Before you write a page, ask: what does the person want to know or do, and does my page help them do it?

What you can influence

You cannot control rankings, but you can improve many things that help search engines and visitors understand your pages.

On the page

  • A clear page title. Give every page a unique, descriptive title.
  • A helpful meta description. It is not a ranking promise, but search engines often use it as the summary shown in results, so it can influence whether people click.
  • One main heading and logical subheadings. Use one H1 per page and organize the rest with H2 and H3 headings.
  • Useful, original content. Answer the real question in your own words and keep it easy to read.
  • Descriptive image alt text. It helps screen-reader users and helps search engines understand images.

On the site

  • Clean, readable URLs. A web address such as /seo-for-beginners is easier to understand than a string of random characters.
  • Internal links. Link related pages together using descriptive link text.
  • Mobile-friendly design. Many people search on their phones.
  • Speed. Lightweight pages load faster and are easier to use.
  • No broken links. Check that your links lead where they should.

Try it yourself: open any page of this website, look at the page title in your browser tab, then scroll and notice the headings. These are the same on-page signals described above.

Common SEO myths

  • "Repeating keywords again and again helps." It makes writing harder to read and does not help you. Write naturally for people.
  • "SEO is a one-time job." Search habits and websites change, so SEO is ongoing.
  • "More pages always means better results." A few helpful pages beat many thin ones.
  • "You can pay to rank in the main results." Search engines offer separate paid advertising, but the regular results are not for sale.
  • "Results appear overnight." New pages usually take time to be found and trusted.

A beginner checklist

  1. Decide what each page is about and who it is for.
  2. Write a unique title and description for every page.
  3. Use one H1 and a clear heading structure.
  4. Write helpful content that matches what people want.
  5. Add descriptive alt text to meaningful images.
  6. Link related pages together.
  7. Publish a sitemap and a robots.txt file.
  8. Check your site on a phone and fix broken links.
  9. Use the free tools that major search engines offer website owners to see how your pages are being crawled and indexed.

Key takeaways and next steps

  • Search engines crawl, index and rank pages.
  • You cannot control rankings, but you can make pages clearer, faster and more useful.
  • Matching search intent matters as much as using the right words.
  • SEO is a patient, ongoing habit.
